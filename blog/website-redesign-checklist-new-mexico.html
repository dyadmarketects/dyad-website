Website Redesign Checklist for New Mexico Small Businesses

By Vincent KingWeb Design & DevelopmentOctober 16, 20266 min read
Quick answer
  • Before anyone touches the design, write down what the site needs to do and save a list of every page you have today.
  • Rebuild page by page around what your customers and team actually need, with real photos and copy written to be found.
  • Protect your Google rankings with 301 redirects from every old URL, then test on phones and watch your numbers after launch.

A good website redesign checklist for a small business comes down to three things: decide what the site needs to do before you think about how it looks, rebuild it page by page around your customers, and protect the search traffic you already have when you launch. Skip the first step and you get a prettier site that doesn't sell. Skip the last one and you can lose the Google rankings that took years to earn.

Below is the checklist we'd walk through with any New Mexico business, whether you're a contractor in Rio Rancho, a shop in Santa Fe or a service company in Albuquerque. Work through it in order. Each step makes the next one easier.

Before you redesign: start with strategy, not looks

Most redesigns start with "our site looks old." That's a fair feeling, but it's a symptom. The real question is what the site should be doing for your business that it isn't doing now.

Vince has a way of putting this. Marketing strategy isn't "the fancy clothes on top of your company." It's "the entire skeletal structure beneath," and it decides how those clothes end up looking. A website is the same. If the structure underneath is right, the design has something solid to stand on.

Your pre-redesign checklist

  • Write down 2 or 3 goals. More calls? More quote requests? Fewer "what are your hours?" phone calls? Easier hiring? Pick the ones that matter most.
  • Know who the site is for. List your main types of customers and what each one is trying to get done when they land on your site.
  • Collect the questions people ask you. The questions you answer on the phone every week are the questions your website should answer first.
  • Look at your competitors. Note what they do well and where they're vague. That's where you can stand out.
  • Gather your logins. Domain registrar, hosting, Google Business Profile, Google Analytics and Google Search Console. Missing access is one of the most common things that slows a launch down.
  • Check your brand. If your logo or colors feel dated, decide whether you need a light refresh or a bigger change before the new site is built. Our post on rebrand vs. refresh can help you sort that out.

Take inventory of your current site

Before anything gets deleted, make a list of every page on your current website and its address (URL). Your sitemap, your website platform and Google Search Console can all help you find them. Include PDFs, menus, forms and any page that people link to.

Then sort each page into one of three groups:

  • Keep and improve. Pages that bring in visitors or leads. These need extra care so you don't lose their traffic.
  • Combine. Thin or overlapping pages that make more sense as one strong page.
  • Retire. Outdated pages, old promos and anything that no longer reflects what you do.

This list becomes your map for launch day, so save it somewhere safe.

Rebuild page by page around real people

This is where a redesign either becomes a real upgrade or just a new coat of paint. Instead of dropping old content into a new template, go through the site one page at a time and ask: who is reading this, and what do they need to do next?

As DSI's Marketing Manager, Vince led a full redesign and launch of the Document Solutions, Inc. website. The site was rebuilt page by page around both the customer experience and the employee experience, with new branding, real team photos and SEO copy built to rank. Thinking about employees as well as customers is easy to miss. Your website is also where future hires check you out and where your own team sends people for answers.

Page by page checklist

  • One clear job per page. Every page should have one main purpose and one obvious next step, like "Call us," "Request a quote" or "Book a visit."
  • Answer the question up top. Put what you do, who it's for and where you work near the top of the page. "Serving Albuquerque, Rio Rancho and Santa Fe" helps visitors and search engines.
  • Use real photos. Your team, your space and your work build trust faster than stock images people have seen a hundred times.
  • Write for how people search. Use the words customers actually use, give each page its own title and description, and don't make two pages compete for the same search.
  • Keep the basics current. Hours, phone number, email, service areas and contact forms should match your Google Business Profile exactly.
  • Make it easy to update. If changing your hours requires a developer, it won't happen. Plan who will maintain the site and how.

Design for phones, speed and everyone

Plenty of your visitors will find you on a phone, often while they're in the car or standing in a parking lot. Design the mobile version first, then scale up to desktop.

  • Tap targets and forms. Buttons should be easy to tap with a thumb, and forms should ask only for what you truly need.
  • Speed. Compress images and avoid heavy add-ons you don't use. Google's Core Web Vitals guidance considers a page's main content loading within 2.5 seconds as "good," along with quick responses to taps and a layout that doesn't jump around.
  • Readable for everyone. Strong color contrast, real text instead of text baked into images, alt text on photos and headings that make sense if someone is using a screen reader.

Launch day: protect your Google rankings

This is the step small businesses skip most often, and it's the one that hurts the most. If your old pages vanish and their addresses change, anyone who clicks an old Google result or bookmark lands on an error page.

Google's own guidance on moving a site is clear on the essentials. Here's the short version as a checklist:

  • Map old URLs to new ones. Use the inventory you made earlier. Every page you kept or combined should point to its closest new match.
  • Set up permanent (301) redirects. Send each old address straight to its new page. Don't dump everything on the homepage, and keep the redirects in place for at least a year.
  • Update your sitemap. Submit the new sitemap in Google Search Console so Google finds the new pages quickly.
  • Remove "don't index" settings. Many sites are built with search engines blocked during development. Make sure that's turned off when you go live.
  • Test every form and button. Send yourself a test message from every form, click every phone number on a phone and check that analytics are recording visits.
  • Update your links elsewhere. Your Google Business Profile, social media bios, email signatures and directory listings should point to the right pages.

Google also notes that rankings can bounce around for a while after a move, so a dip in the first few weeks isn't a reason to panic. It's a reason to keep an eye on things.

After launch: keep improving

A launch is the start, not the finish line. In the first couple of months:

  • Check Search Console for pages Google can't find and fix any missed redirects.
  • Watch which pages people visit, where they leave and which ones lead to calls or form submissions.
  • Ask a few customers to use the site on their phone and tell you what was confusing.
  • Add fresh photos, answer new customer questions and keep your hours and services up to date.

Redesign or just update?

Not every dated site needs a full rebuild. If yours works well on phones, loads quickly, is easy to edit and still brings in leads, new copy and new photos might be all you need. If it's slow, hard to use on a phone, hard to update or no longer reflects what your business does, a page by page redesign usually makes more sense than patching it.

Frequently asked questions

Will redesigning my website hurt my Google rankings?

It can if pages disappear or change addresses without a plan. Google recommends mapping every old URL to its new one and using permanent 301 redirects, then submitting an updated sitemap. Expect some temporary ups and downs while Google catches up.

Should I redesign my website or just update it?

If your site still works on phones, loads quickly and brings in leads, a content and photo update may be enough. If it's hard to use on a phone, slow, hard to edit or no longer matches what your business does, a page by page redesign usually pays off.

What should I have ready before a website redesign?

A short list of goals, a list of every page on your current site, access to your domain, hosting and Google accounts, your logo files, and real photos of your team, place and work. Notes on the questions customers ask you most are gold for writing the new copy.
